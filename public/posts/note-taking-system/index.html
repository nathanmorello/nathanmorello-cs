Start Anew#

I’m not new to learning, and I’m certainly not new to writing. I come from the academic world — from a field most people would politely describe as “challenging” (ancient history and languages). I’ve published specialist articles and even a book. But I have zero experience in what is now called “content creation.”

Over the past few years, however, I’ve questioned everything: I changed careers, started working with computers — something my friends and family still have trouble believing — and now I’m diving into DevOps and documenting the journey on this blog.

This new phase marks another turning point. I want to use it not only as a learning journal, but also as a playground to test the techniques I’m studying — along with others I’ve carried with me from past lives — while trying to break free from the mental autopilot I’ve built over the years.

In the articles under the category “How I Make This Blog,” I plan to write about all aspects of the process behind building this site. Soon I’ll publish purely technical articles about how I run the blog with Hugo and how I host it. But I also want to outline the context and the methods I use to take notes and create content.

This article is about the quest for the perfect note-taking system — something that I, and many others in my situation, truly need. What you’ll find here is the system I’ve refined over the past few months. It wasn’t easy at first, especially trying to make the PARA method coexist with the Zettelkasten. But after several weeks of calibrating, adjusting, and understanding the logic behind what I was doing, the machine is now working very well, and I feel ready to share it — and even recommend it to others.

The Ultimate Goal: Having a Structure instead of a Plan#

Easy, fast… and lasting: it’s possible!

The real issue — the quest — is simple: you get distracted easily. Work, daily life, small children… it’s incredibly easy to lose track of whatever you’re studying or just sparked your curiosity five minutes earlier.

Especially for someone like me: new career, thousands of new things to learn, and very little that can be reused from my previous knowledge. When obligations start filling not only your time but also your mental RAM, it’s not unusual to walk into a room and suddenly wonder why you’re there, why you stood up in the first place, or where that vase on the table came from.

And if you can’t hold onto that, imagine trying to maintain the famous “momentum.” Because most of the time, at least for me, if you don’t capture ideas while they happen, they’re gone.

What you need is a structure, instead of a plan. A plan is what most of us learned in school and university: make a plan! stick to the plan!… and inevitably: you’re behind on the plan!

One of the parts that most fascinated me of the excellent How To Take Smart Notes is when its author, Sönke Ahrens, explains that with the right note-taking system you gain not only good notes, but — more importantly — a good structure. A structure is a place where things don’t get lost or disappear into the fog of “what was I even thinking when I read that book?”. Instead, it’s a place where pieces come together easily, where you can recall ideas, and where information can be transformed into something new.

StructurePlan
Supports youControls you
FlexibleRigid
Adapts to changeBreaks when life changes
Helps you restart quicklyPunishes you when you fall behind

A plan controls you. You either follow it or you fail it; and “failing to follow the plan” becomes your destiny.

A structure, on the other hand, supports you: whenever you return to a topic, everything you need is already there. You don’t start from zero. You don’t lose the thread.

With a structure, you regain freedom — and momentum.

Paraphrasing Hannibal Smith: “I love it when a plan structure comes together.”

To realize such structure in the past months I have been watching a lot of videos, reading two books and practicing the two following methods in particular:

  • The PARA method of digital knowledge management, adapted my way

  • The note-taking principles of the so-called Zettelkasten, or slip-box.

  • In this other article you can find a description of how I use Obsidian, Markdown, and GitHub to keep thias system efficient.

i-PARA-Z#

PARA is a digital organization system created by Tiago Forte and described in his well-known book Building a Second Brain. Part of its success comes from how lightweight it is. No matter your level of experience, you can’t really get it wrong.

You take a device, the Document folder in your computer or your Google Drive, you grab everything there is and drop it into a folder called Archive (the final “A” in PARA), and… start fresh.

Then you create at least three folders:

FolderMeaningPurpose
ProjectsActive workThings with a deadline or outcome
AreasOngoing roles/responsibilitiesHealth, career, finances, parenting, etc.
ResourcesLearning materialBooks, articles, podcasts, the so-called “literary notes”
Inbox(Optional but recommended)A dumping ground for new notes

PARA works because it forces you to simplify first, organize later.

And what about the Archive? Well, I find that part really interesting. Tiago Forte explains in his books that this idea of throwing everything in and starting fresh was one of the last things he realized about the method.

For a long time, he spent a lot of time and energy helping his students reorganize their old material so that it would align with the new system. But in reality, it was never perfect: you will always have to adjust, move things around, delete… And the effort you put into it drains your energy—and you end up forgetting where things are.

Using the Archive as a reset button, on the other hand, gives you the fresh perspective and clarity you need to start new projects and make use of old material. If you need something, you will remember it, and you’ll know where to put it.

Smart Notes and the Zettelkasten#

According to Tiago Forte, PARA should apply across all your digital spaces — your note-taking app, your computer’s Documents folder, your Google Drive.

In my case, the first place I implemented PARA is Obsidian, my main note-taking system. There, my PARA became i-PARA (thanks to the Inbox). And with the addition of one more essential folder — the Zettelkasten — it evolved into i-PARA-Z.

The Zettelkasten is where the most important notes live: the ones that fuel creativity, knowledge building, and ultimately the articles published here.

The method was developed by Niklas Luhmann and is a modern reinterpretation of the traditional slip-box system. It focuses on quickly transforming reading notes — from books, articles, videos, podcasts, anything — into connected, polished ideas.

If you will care to read a great book How To Take Smart Notes, by Sönke Ahrens, you will learn everything you need to know about this guy and on how he could be so extremely prolific and inventive thanks (also) to his method.

The Three Types of Notes#

The Zettelkasten method consist of three main kind of notes

Fleeting Notes#

These are quick, rough notes — thoughts you write down while reading or listening to something. The key is to “live with a pen in hand,” or at least with a note app on your phone.

Fleeting notes don’t need to be polished; they just need to capture your thinking before it evaporates.

Write anything that your future self might thank you for — even if it looks stupid right now.

Ideally, within a couple of days (in practice, for me, it’s often a couple of weeks…) fleeting notes should become permanent notes.

And that’s okay. Sometimes letting time pass helps you see more clearly what’s worth keeping.

Literature Notes#

These are bibliographic notes — the references and highlights you take while reading.

And they’re not just for books. Take literature notes for videos, songs, podcasts, articles — anything that triggers your thinking.

You don’t take notes to remember the source. You take notes to remember how the source changed your thinking.

Permanent Notes#

Permanent notes are meant to be the building blocks of your entire knowledge system. They’re grammatically clean and ready to reuse, each one capturing a single idea in a short, standalone format that makes sense even without context. You write them in your own words to avoid copy-paste thinking, and you only include quotations when they’re truly necessary. This simplicity and clarity is what allows permanent notes to scale into larger structures—articles, projects, and anything else you create.

TraitMeaning
Grammatically correctReady to reuse
StandaloneUnderstandable without context
ShortOne idea per note
In your own wordsNo copy–paste thinking
Rarely quotationsOnly when necessary

They’re the building blocks of everything else — including the article you’re reading now.

Conclusion#

The outcome of all these notes is a flexible, living archive — not a collection of random annotations, but paragraphs already shaped and ready to be combined, rearranged, or expanded into new ideas.

Most importantly, the Zettelkasten together with the PARA system, give you the structure you are looking for.
